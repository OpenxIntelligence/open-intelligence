# Open Intelligence: initial patient journey and capability research

**Research date: 2 November 2026. Scope:** six public patient/caregiver narratives, used for product discovery. No medical records, data archives, genomes, images or patient datasets were opened or acquired; no synthetic patient cases were derived. Each observed account remains separate from the proposed capabilities below. No outcome is attributed to AI.

**Main finding:** build a shared, reviewable navigation workspace around the evidence report. These accounts expose work involving referrals, care transitions, practical feasibility, unanswered questions and caregiver capacity. They do not establish how common those problems are or whether software would resolve them.

## 1. Lacey: rare metastatic solid tumor and specialist access

**Observed journey.** After monitoring paragangliomas, Lacey learned in 2023 that they had spread. Several physicians recommended observation given the circumstances described. Through Pheo Para Alliance support groups, she reached an NIH investigator and joined a trial in 2024. The article does not establish that the trial replaced observation or improved her outcome.

**Information and barriers.** The narrative illustrates the importance of specialist access through peer networks. Product-discovery questions include how patients understand observation, what would prompt reassessment, and the distinction between treatment trials and other research.

**Capability opportunity.** Preserve each clinician's rationale, link applicable evidence and uncertainties, prepare questions for review, and maintain a verified specialist/referral task with an owner. Extending the described approach means preserving context and follow-through across consultations.

**Human boundary.** Do not overrule observation, promise benefit, establish eligibility or infer an indicated intervention.

**Evidence:** NCI CCR, 13 December 2024, institution-edited patient narrative. Trial type and later outcome unspecified; research-participation selection bias. [Source](https://ccr.cancer.gov/news/article/empowering-others-to-contribute-to-rare-neuroendocrine-tumor-research).

## 2. Luis: hematologic cancer, recurrence and cross-border coordination

**Observed journey.** Luis describes diagnosis delayed during the pandemic, recurrent lymphoma, and seeking help through social media, including encounters with scammers. NIH specialists refined his diagnosis. Trial opening, donor availability, transplant preparation and his mother's availability as a full-time caregiver had to align. Recurrence required a second transplant; limited nearby specialist care affected how long he stayed near NIH.

**Information and barriers.** Needed coordination crossed countries, specialists, eligibility, donor services, caregivers and time. The account shows why a search result alone is not access.

**Capability opportunity.** Maintain a verified referral directory, distinguish possible trial fit from confirmed eligibility, expose missing information, and coordinate a clinician-reviewed readiness checklist. Record recurrence as a new review event while retaining earlier decisions and uncertainty.

**Human boundary.** Diagnosis, transplant/donor decisions, consent, travel feasibility and urgent deterioration require professionals; popularity of an online recommendation is not evidence.

**Evidence:** NCI CCR, 6 October 2023, patient and clinician account. A highly selected research-center pathway; later status unverified. [Source](https://ccr.cancer.gov/news/article/the-ups-and-downs-of-a-rare-blood-cancer-wont-stop-one-resilient-patient).

## 3. Tina and her parents: pediatric referral and family burden

**Observed journey.** Tina's parents sought pediatric care, then hospital reassessment when symptoms persisted. After a brain-tumor diagnosis, she was referred to St. Jude. Her father's account emphasizes the relief of having treatment, travel, housing and food supported, alongside specialist care and subsequent follow-up.

**Information and barriers.** Families must understand the next step while managing fear, appointments and practical needs. The account supports studying family coordination; it does not show what information these parents lacked at each visit.

**Capability opportunity.** A caregiver-controlled workspace could hold a plain-language appointment brief, confirmed contacts, next actions, practical-support status and questions to discuss with the team. Extend the described support by keeping the family and clinical review aligned between visits.

**Human boundary.** Symptom assessment, diagnosis, treatment, pediatric consent/assent and safeguarding cannot be delegated to the platform. A billing model at one center cannot be promised elsewhere.

**Evidence:** St. Jude, undated page; diagnosis in 2017 and arrival in January 2018. Parent/patient quotes within a fundraising narrative; unusually comprehensive institutional support. [Source](https://www.stjude.org/about-st-jude/stories/meet-our-patients/meet-tina.html).

## 4. Kellie: rural access to a clinical trial

**Observed journey.** Kellie's oncologists offered an NCORP trial for her lung cancer. Her visits were available at a local hospital, about 20 minutes away. The account contrasts that access with travel of hundreds of miles to a major center and describes the local oncologist and research coordinator's roles.

**Information and barriers.** Questions include trial purpose and randomization, required visits, care location, family/work demands and local availability. Practical feasibility can determine whether an option can be considered.

**Capability opportunity.** Present potential studies with site-confirmation dates, questions for the coordinator, travel/visit requirements and known versus unknown cost coverage. Connect national evidence to local delivery options. Extend the described method with a reusable, reviewable feasibility comparison.

**Human boundary.** Staff confirm open slots and eligibility; clinicians and patients decide whether participation is appropriate. Distance must not become an automatic exclusion rule.

**Evidence:** NCI, 20 September 2023, institutional patient/research-coordinator story. Documents a functioning local network; does not describe everyone who lacked access. [Source](https://www.cancer.gov/research/participate/stories/clinical-trial-rural-america).

## 5. Brittany: second opinion and lifelong care transitions

**Observed journey.** Brittany describes six months of appointments before a teenage thyroid-cancer diagnosis. Local doctors recommended a second opinion; her parents coordinated travel and care in another state. Later education and family moves required establishing new providers. She also describes lasting functional needs and creating age-appropriate peer support that she had wanted earlier.

**Information and barriers.** Who understands the history, who owns follow-up, what functional needs persist, and how does caregiver participation change as a young person becomes an adult?

**Capability opportunity.** A patient-approved, clinician-reviewed handoff brief could preserve historical decisions, continuing needs, unanswered questions and the next responsible professional. Track referral acknowledgment and shared-review completion; support changing permissions and age-appropriate resources.

**Human boundary.** Do not prescribe surveillance, equate old needs with current status, or transfer parental access automatically. Clinical judgment and the person's choices remain central.

**Evidence:** NCI DCCPS, undated public survivor narrative. Retrospective account of a highly engaged advocate with substantial family support; not representative of unsupported transitions. [Source](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/ocs/resources/survivor-caregiver-stories/brittany).

## 6. Sarah and Lynda: care fragmentation, housing and an unmet final wish

**Observed journey.** Sarah reports insufficient answers after her mother's advanced pancreatic-cancer diagnosis, unsafe housing, an hour's travel, employment and sole-caregiver responsibilities. She used charity information and a diary. Lynda was too ill to answer a specialist nurse's email. A desired move nearer Sarah did not happen before she died; a nursing-home place was found shortly before the planned discharge.

**Information and barriers.** The limiting factors included capacity, communication, housing and ownership of next steps. Income is not reported; this is not evidence of a particular socioeconomic classification.

**Capability opportunity.** Offer short caregiver-authorized updates, call-back requests, a shared issue log and named human ownership of medical, social-care and housing tasks. Extend the diary into tracked follow-through, including a change in goals toward comfort and proximity to family.

**Human boundary.** Software cannot guarantee placement, resolve unsafe housing, determine prognosis or claim that earlier intervention would have prevented death.

**Evidence:** Pancreatic Cancer UK, 7 April 2026 (archive date); daughter's retrospective account, mother died July 2025. Care shortcomings are her account, not independently adjudicated. [Source](https://www.pancreaticcancer.org.uk/real-life-stories/sarah-lynda/).

## Priorities for the MVP

These are **design hypotheses**, grounded in the accounts above, to test with patients, caregivers, clinicians and navigators.

| Priority | Proposed workflow and added value | Evidence to seek in discovery |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Reviewed context and questions** | Capture authorized goals, preferences, practical constraints, a dated narrative and unresolved questions. Distinguish patient report, document-supported fact and clinical interpretation. Generate a short consultation brief. | Can a person correct the brief and identify its uncertainties? Does it reduce repeated explanation without hiding material context? |
| **2. Evidence comparison for shared review** | Compare supporting/contradicting evidence, applicability, access limits and uncertainty; record why an option, observation or supportive-care discussion is being considered. Link the reviewer decision to the evidence version. | Can reviewers locate the support and disagreement, identify missing evidence and reject an inappropriate interpretation? |
| **3. Practical access alongside scientific fit** | Present a potential referral/trial with location, verified contact, visit burden, caregiver needs, funding unknowns and next confirmation task. Permit preferences without silently excluding options. | Which apparent options fail at contact, cost, transport, staffing or eligibility confirmation, and why? |
| **4. Named ownership and caregiver delegation** | Turn questions into tasks with an owner, due date, status and escalation route; support granular caregiver access and low-effort updates. Draft communications for authorized human review. | Do unanswered requests surface? Can exhausted caregivers use the workflow? Does it create extra work for clinical teams? |
| **5. Versioned reassessment and handoffs** | Record new context or evidence, show what changed, request human review, and preserve earlier reports. Produce a handoff brief and confirm receipt when care changes. | Are consequential changes reviewed promptly? Are alerts useful, and are ownership gaps resolved? |

The immediate loop is: **authorized context → permitted evidence → explicit comparison → source-linked brief → human review → owned next actions → versioned reassessment**. Measure reviewability, corrections, task completion and burden before considering clinical-outcome claims. Do not convert anecdotal pathways into treatment rules.

## Evidence quality, exclusions and next research

This is purposive, English-language desk research, not interviews or a representative sample. Four sources are NCI accounts, one a hospital fundraising page, and one a charity-published caregiver account. Institutional editing, recall, selection and survivorship biases are substantial. Facts stop at each article's date; current status was not checked. NCI CCR pages returned 403 on direct opening; their full public narrative text and dates were available in indexed official-source search results. No linked clinical files were followed.

The set spans rare/advanced solid disease, hematologic disease, pediatric caregiving, rural/cross-border access, second opinions, clinical trials, survivorship and unresolved end-of-life barriers. It does **not** establish prevalence, comparative effectiveness or software benefit. Sid's records and data catalog were not accessed; these six narratives were sufficient for this initial scope.

Next, validate the hypotheses through separately consented interviews, deliberately including people who declined or could not access trials, people without caregivers, limited-English/digital-access users, and people facing financial hardship. Interview navigators, social workers, community oncologists and palliative-care professionals about responsibility and service capacity. Use anonymous process descriptions and task walkthroughs; acquiring medical records is unnecessary for that discovery phase. All six sources were retrieved on **2 November 2026**.
